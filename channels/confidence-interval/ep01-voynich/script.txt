So the book is medieval. Does the text actually say something?
Languages have a fingerprint. In every human language, a few words are extremely common and most are rare, in a very specific mathematical ratio. It's called Zipf's law. Voynichese follows it.
In 2013, physicists Marcelo Montemurro and Damián Zanette went further. They looked at where words appear across the whole book. In real texts, topic words cluster: you see "leaf" and "root" in the botany chapter, not in the astronomy one. They found that same kind of clustering in the Voynich manuscript, with words tied to its illustrated sections.
Their conclusion: this structure supports the presence of a genuine message.
That's real evidence. Gibberish drops. Both "meaningful" explanations rise.
But here's where I have to argue against myself.
Voynichese is also strangely too regular. Some words repeat two or three times in a row, which almost never happens in real languages. Words are built from a tiny set of predictable parts, like a machine assembling them.
In 2004, researcher Gordon Rugg showed that a simple tool available in the 1500s, a card with holes cut in it called a Cardan grille, could churn out text with many of these quirks.
And in 2020, Torsten Timm and Andreas Schinner proposed something even simpler. The writer just copies words they've already written nearby, changes a letter or two, and keeps going. They call it "self-citation." No secret language needed, no math, no plan. And it reproduces a surprising amount of the book's statistics, including, they argue, the patterns that make it look like language.
This hurts. Because it means the evidence I just counted for "meaningful" can be produced without meaning.

Next clue. Not the words. The handwriting.
In 2020, medieval manuscript expert Lisa Fagin Davis compared the letter shapes across the whole book and concluded it was written by five different scribes.
Think about what that means for the gibberish theory. One bored prankster is easy to imagine. Five people, carefully producing two hundred and forty pages of nonsense that stays consistent across all of them, is harder. Not impossible, a workshop could do it, but harder.

Now the new evidence. The reason this video exists.
A long-standing objection to the cipher theory is that Voynichese doesn't look like any known medieval cipher. Real ciphers of the time tended to scramble the text into something that no longer behaved like words.
In a paper published in the journal Cryptologia and widely covered in January 2026, Michael Greshko built one that does. He calls it the Naibbe cipher, after a medieval Italian card game. You take Latin or Italian text, break it into short pieces, and swap each piece using tables, choosing between options by rolling dice and drawing playing cards. Everything a fifteenth-century writer could have had on the table.
The output reproduces many of the manuscript's quirks: symbol frequencies, word lengths, where certain glyphs like to sit inside a word.
Greshko is careful to say this doesn't solve the manuscript. And neither do I. What it does is remove an argument. "No real cipher could look like this" is no longer true.
And then, in October 2026, this.
Lisa Fagin Davis, together with computer scientist Colin Layfield at the University of Malta, published a study in Digital Medievalist. Davis had noticed something odd while tracking her five scribes: the handwriting switches back and forth from page to page in ways that make no sense for how books were made.
Layfield compared blocks of text across pages using a statistical method called latent semantic analysis. Their conclusion is that the book was never meant to be bound the way it is now. It was originally loose, folded sheets, each one written by a single scribe, read in sequence. Somebody later bound them in the wrong order.
