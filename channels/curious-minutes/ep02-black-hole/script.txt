Imagine you fall into a black hole.
Not a movie black hole. A real one, the size of the one at the center of our galaxy.
Would you be stretched like spaghetti? Would time stop? Would you see anything at all?
In 2024, NASA ran this exact trip on a supercomputer. And the answer depends on one thing you'd never guess: how big the black hole is.
Pick the wrong one, and you're torn apart before you even reach it. Pick the right one, and you get to see the edge with your own eyes.
Let's take the trip.
First, what are we falling into?
A black hole is a place where gravity is so strong that nothing gets out. Not rockets. Not even light.
Its edge is called the event horizon. Inside it, the speed you'd need to escape is faster than light. And nothing goes faster than light.
So whatever crosses that line stays inside. Forever.
That's why black holes are black. They don't shine. They don't reflect anything.
So how do we know they're there? Because of what's around them.
Gas that falls toward a black hole settles into a hot, bright, spinning disk. That glowing disk is how we see them.
Black holes come in very different sizes.
The small ones are called stellar-mass black holes. When a star with around twenty times the Sun's mass or more runs out of fuel, its core collapses into one.
NASA says there may be as many as a hundred million of them in our galaxy alone.
The big ones are called supermassive. Almost every large galaxy has one at its center. Including ours.
Ours is called Sagittarius A star. It's about four million times the mass of the Sun, and about twenty-seven thousand light-years away.
That sounds huge. But it's actually on the small side for a supermassive black hole.
The one at the center of a galaxy called Holmberg fifteen A holds at least forty billion times the mass of the Sun.
In 2022, astronomers showed the first image of it. To take it, they linked eight radio observatories across the planet into one Earth-sized telescope.
From here, it looks about as big as a doughnut on the Moon.
And what they saw was a dark shadow, wrapped in a bright ring of glowing gas.
That shadow is roughly twice as big as the black hole's actual surface, because the black hole bends the light passing around it.
And it was a hard picture to take. The gas around our black hole circles it in mere minutes, so the view kept changing while the telescopes were watching. The final image is an average of many.
Three years earlier, the same team had shown the first image of any black hole: M87 star, in another galaxy. It's more than a thousand times bigger than ours. And yet, up close, the two look remarkably similar.
That's our destination.
Now, the trip.
NASA astrophysicist Jeremy Schnittman wanted to show what falling into a black hole would really look like.
So he and Brian Powell simulated it on NASA's Discover supercomputer.
The black hole in their simulation weighs four point three million Suns. Just like the one at the center of the Milky Way.
The run made about ten terabytes of data. It took about five days, using just zero point three percent of the machine's processors.
On a typical laptop, NASA says, it would have taken more than a decade.
In the simulation, a camera stands in for a daring astronaut. So, for the next few minutes, that camera is you.
You start nearly four hundred million miles away. That's about six hundred forty million kilometers.
The black hole is already filling your view.
Around it, the glowing disk of gas. And closer in, something stranger: thin rings of light.
These are called photon rings. They're made of light that has gone around the black hole one or more times before escaping to you.
The black hole's gravity bends light so much that you can see the same disk more than once.
And one side of the disk looks brighter than the other. Near the black hole, the gas spins so fast that the side coming toward you looks brighter and bluer, and the side moving away looks dimmer and redder.
As you get closer, the event horizon comes into view. In this black hole, it spans about sixteen million miles. That's about seventeen percent of the distance from Earth to the Sun.
You are not falling straight in.
In real time, the fall to the event horizon takes about three hours. And along the way, you swing around the black hole almost twice, each orbit taking about thirty minutes.
You're speeding up the whole time.
At one point, about seventy million miles from the horizon, you're already moving at nineteen percent of the speed of light. That's nearly a hundred twenty-seven million miles an hour.
And the faster you go, the weirder the sky looks.
Light from straight ahead gets brighter and whiter. It's like the sound of a race car rising in pitch as it comes toward you.
And the whole sky seems to slide forward, toward the direction you're moving. The sky is shrinking in front of your eyes.
Now let's leave a friend behind. Someone floating safely, far away, watching you fall.
For you, nothing seems unusual. Your watch seems to tick like always.
But for your friend, something very strange happens.
Near a black hole, space and time get so distorted that your friend sees you slow down. You get slower, and slower.
And then, just before the edge, you seem to freeze. Your friend never actually sees you cross.
This is why astronomers once called black holes "frozen stars."
But you don't freeze. You keep falling.
And then you cross the event horizon.
At the horizon, even space itself is flowing inward at the speed of light. There is no turning back.
Light from the outside universe still shines in. But nothing can ever get out. Not your radio. Not your light. Nothing.
Now, the part everyone asks about.
Schnittman put it this way: once the camera crosses the horizon, its destruction by spaghettification is just twelve point eight seconds away.
Spaghettification is a real scientific word. Here's what it means.
Gravity pulls harder on things that are closer. So when you're falling feet first, your feet are pulled harder than your head.
The difference is tiny on Earth. Near a black hole, it becomes enormous.
You get stretched long and thin. Like a noodle.
In NASA's simulation, from the moment the camera is destroyed, it's only about seventy-nine thousand five hundred miles to the very center. That last stretch takes microseconds.
And this is where the size of the black hole matters most.
You might think a smaller black hole would be safer. It's the opposite.
Schnittman says stellar-mass black holes, the small ones up to about thirty times the Sun's mass, have much smaller event horizons and stronger tidal forces.
Those forces can rip objects apart before they even reach the horizon.
So with a small black hole, you'd be spaghettified outside. You'd never even get to see the edge.
With a supermassive one, the stretching is gentler at the horizon. You cross the line in one piece, and the end comes later, inside.
In his words: if you have the choice, you want to fall into a supermassive black hole.
Is spaghettification just theory? No. Astronomers have watched it happen.
In 2020, ESO announced that its telescopes, along with others around the world, had recorded a star being torn apart by a supermassive black hole.
It was just over two hundred fifteen million light-years away, the closest flare of its kind recorded at the time.
As one of the researchers explained, the black hole's gravity shredded the star into thin streams of material.
The star had roughly the same mass as our Sun. It lost about half of that to the black hole, which is over a million times more massive.
And while it fed, the black hole blasted debris outward at up to ten thousand kilometers per second.
Astronomers watched the whole flare rise and fade over six months.
So the noodles are real. Just on a much bigger scale.
But what if you don't fall in?
NASA simulated that too.
In the second scenario, the camera swings close to the event horizon, but never crosses it. It slingshots back out, to safety.
Imagine an astronaut flying that six-hour round trip, while her crew waits on a mothership far away.
When she gets back, she's thirty-six minutes younger than her crewmates.
That's not a trick of the camera. Time really passes more slowly near a strong source of gravity, and when you move close to the speed of light.
And Schnittman says it can get even more extreme. If the black hole were spinning fast, like the one in the 2014 movie Interstellar, she'd come back many years younger than her shipmates.
So the black hole would not just bend light. It would bend your time.
And what's at the very bottom?
At the center of a black hole, general relativity predicts a singularity. A point where matter is crushed to infinite density.
It's where the laws of physics as we know them stop working.
NASA says the singularity may be a real physical thing, or just a mathematical one. Right now, astronomers don't know which.
So nobody can tell you what's at the bottom. Not yet.
