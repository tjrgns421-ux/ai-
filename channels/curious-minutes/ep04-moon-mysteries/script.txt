You'd never expect a hut on the Moon.
But in 2021, a rover drove a month to reach one. What was it, actually?
That's the first of six strange things found on the Moon.
For each one, we'll look at what was actually found, and give an honest verdict: solved, best guess, or still unknown.
Number one: the hut on the far side.
In December 2021, China's Yutu 2 rover was driving across the far side of the Moon, the side that always faces away from Earth.
Yutu 2 had landed there in January 2019, inside a huge crater called Von Kármán, as part of the Chang'e 4 mission. It was the first soft landing ever made on the far side.
On the horizon, its cameras caught something odd. A blur shaped like a cube, with a strange flat top.
The rover's own outreach team, called Our Space, joked that it might be the hut of alien pioneers.
The picture went around the world.
Because there was nothing next to it for scale, many people imagined something huge. Some pictured a building like the Arc de Triomphe.
But not everyone was excited. Lunar scientist Philip Stooke said it was just a rock on a crater rim. In his words: it won't look like a hut.
He even found the rock in pictures taken from orbit by NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter. Scientifically, he said, the rock could still be interesting.
There was only one way to find out. Drive there.
The trip took about a month.
And in January 2022, the close-up pictures came back.
The hut was a rock. A much smaller rock than it had looked from far away, and much rounder.
In fact, people thought it looked like a little rabbit, crouched over a couple of carrots, which were really just smaller rocks.
The team nicknamed it jade rabbit. And here's the funny part: that's exactly what the rover's name, Yutu, means.
As one space journalist put it, the Moon's surface is thirty-eight million square kilometers of rocks. It would have been astronomically exceptional for it to be anything else.
Verdict: solved. It was a rock.
Number two: the gel in the crater.
This one was found by the same rover, two years earlier.
In July 2019, a member of the rover's driving team spotted something shiny in a small crater.
The first Chinese report described it with a word that can be translated as gel-like.
A gel, on the dry, airless Moon? With no pictures at first, the story spread fast, and so did the speculation.
Some scientists guessed early on that it was probably glass, melted by a meteorite impact. But guessing isn't the same as checking.
Then the scientists took a closer look.
In 2020, a team led by Gou Sheng of the Chinese Academy of Sciences published an analysis, using the rover's cameras and its spectrometer.
Their answer: it's not a gel. It's a dark, greenish, glistening rock called an impact melt breccia, about fifty-two by sixteen centimeters.
When a meteorite slams into the Moon, the heat can melt rock and dust and weld the pieces together. The shine likely comes from glass.
And it looks a lot like samples the Apollo astronauts brought home, including one collected by geologist Harrison Schmitt.
That sample is made of dark, broken pieces of minerals cemented together, with black, shiny glass.
A NASA scientist called it very inspiring that new missions are finding features on the far side that look like what the Apollo astronauts saw.
The researchers were careful to say the results are not definitive. The lighting was bad, and nobody has a sample from that spot.
Still, every piece of evidence points the same way.
Verdict: solved. It's rock and glass, melted by an old impact.
Number three: the Moon that rang.
In November 1969, Apollo 12 left a seismometer on the Moon, an instrument that feels the ground shake.
After the astronauts left the surface, NASA sent the empty top half of their lunar module crashing back into the Moon on purpose, to see what the shaking would look like.
It hit at about six thousand kilometers an hour and left a crater around nine meters wide.
On Earth, the shaking from an impact like that would fade quickly.
On the Moon, it kept going. The ground vibrated for more than fifty-five minutes. The signal was totally unlike anything the scientists had seen before.
Even after an hour, the smallest vibrations had not completely stopped.
It's easy to see why people said the Moon rang like a bell.
So, is the Moon hollow? No.
The reason is much simpler. On Earth, many things soak up the shaking and calm it down. On the Moon, there is much less to absorb it. So the vibrations keep going, long after the impact is over.
Moonquakes work the same way. Even natural ones can shake for up to an hour.
Verdict: solved. The Moon isn't hollow. It just doesn't calm down.
Number four: the Moon is rusting.
Rust is what you get when iron meets oxygen and water. Mars is red because of it.
But the Moon has no air to speak of, and very little water. So in 2020, scientists were surprised to find signs of rust on it anyway.
The clue came from an instrument called the Moon Mineralogy Mapper, built by NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, flying on India's Chandrayaan-1 orbiter.
Researcher Shuai Li was studying light reflected from the Moon's poles, and found the signature of hematite, a form of rust.
One of his colleagues said: at first, I totally didn't believe it. It shouldn't exist based on the conditions present on the Moon.
It gets stranger. The Sun constantly sprays the Moon with hydrogen in the solar wind, and hydrogen works against rust.
So where would the oxygen come from?
The best explanation points back at us.
Earth's magnetic field trails behind our planet like a windsock. And oxygen from Earth's upper atmosphere can ride along it, all the way to the Moon, about three hundred eighty-five thousand kilometers away.
That fits a pattern in the data: there's more rust on the side of the Moon that faces Earth than on the far side.
And there's a bonus. During the full Moon, Earth's magnetic tail blocks more than ninety-nine percent of the solar wind. That opens a window when rust can form.
But there's a loose end. Smaller amounts of rust are also forming on the far side, where Earth's oxygen shouldn't be able to reach.
There's one more twist. The Moon has been slowly drifting away from Earth for billions of years. So long ago, when the two were closer, even more oxygen may have made the trip.
Verdict: best guess. Oxygen from Earth is the leading explanation, but it's not proven yet.
Number five: the swirls.
On the Moon, there are bright, twisting patterns that look like someone painted them. They're called lunar swirls, and they're found nowhere else.
The most famous one is called Reiner Gamma. You can see it from Earth with a backyard telescope.
From orbit, NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter shows its tendrils stretching for several hundred kilometers.
For a long time, no one could explain them.
Here's the current best answer. In some places, the Moon's crust is slightly magnetic. Those small magnetic patches deflect the solar wind, like sunscreen.
The protected ground stays bright. The particles that get pushed aside darken the ground around it.
NASA describes the swirls as the Moon's tan lines.
Verdict: best guess. Recent models and spacecraft data support it, but the details are still being worked out.
Number six: the Moon has two faces.
The side of the Moon we see is covered in big dark patches, called maria, or seas. They're old lava plains.
For most of history, nobody had seen the other side.
Because the Moon always keeps the same face toward us, about eighty-two percent of the far side stayed hidden from Earth until 1959.
By the way, it's not dark. People sometimes call it the dark side, but it gets just as much sunlight as the near side. It's only dark to us, because we can't see it.
Then, on the seventh of October, 1959, the Soviet probe Luna 3 took the first photos of the far side.
And it looked like a different world. Rugged, battered, covered in craters, with almost no dark seas.
The first humans to see it with their own eyes were the Apollo 8 astronauts, in December 1968.
Today we can measure it. Only one percent of the far side is covered by those dark plains, compared to about thirty-one percent of the near side.
Why would one world have two such different faces?
There are several ideas. One says the near side had more heat-producing elements inside, so more lava reached the surface.
Another says that when the Moon was young, the still-hot Earth heated the near side, so the far side cooled first and grew a thicker crust. Impacts on the near side could break through its thinner crust, and lava flooded out.
In 2019, China's Chang'e 4 made the first landing on the far side. And in June 2024, Chang'e 6 brought home the first samples ever collected from it.
Those samples may finally help settle the question.
Verdict: still unknown. There are good ideas, but no final answer yet.
So, let's check the scoreboard.
The hut: solved. It was a rock.
The gel: solved. Rock and glass from an old impact.
The ringing Moon: solved. It's not hollow, it just doesn't calm down.
The rust: best guess. Oxygen from Earth.
The swirls: best guess. Magnetic sunscreen.
The two faces: still unknown.
Three solved, two best guesses, and one we still can't explain. After more than sixty years of missions, the Moon still has secrets.
