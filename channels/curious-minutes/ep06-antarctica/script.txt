You'd never expect a pyramid in Antarctica.
But this 1,265-meter peak has four steep sides. What is it, actually?
That's the first of six strange things found in Antarctica.
Antarctica attracts a lot of wild theories online. So for each one, we'll look at what was actually found, and give an honest verdict: solved, best guess, or still unknown.
Number one: the pyramid.
In 2016, pictures of a mountain in Antarctica went viral. Seen from above, it looked strikingly like an Egyptian pyramid.
Some people said a lost civilization built it. Others said aliens.
Here's what we know. The peak is in the Ellsworth Mountains, a range first spotted from a plane by the American aviator Lincoln Ellsworth in 1935.
It's about 1,265 meters tall, roughly one-fifth the height of Denali, the tallest mountain in North America.
It doesn't even have an official name. And it isn't hidden. A research camp sits just south of it.
So how do you get four steep sides? Erosion.
Water gets into cracks in the rock, freezes, and expands. Over and over. The cracks grow, and pieces break off. Over hundreds of millions of years, that can carve a mountain into sharp faces.
Geologist Eric Rignot put it simply: this is just a mountain that looks like a pyramid.
He also pointed out that many peaks have one or two faces like that. Four is rare. But rare isn't impossible.
Verdict: solved. It's a mountain.
Number two: the waterfall of blood.
In the McMurdo Dry Valleys, a glacier called Taylor Glacier has a dark red stain pouring out of its face. It's called Blood Falls.
It was found in 1911 by the geologist Thomas Griffith Taylor. Back then, the red color was blamed on red algae.
But that wasn't it. And there was a second puzzle. The average temperature there is about minus seventeen degrees Celsius. So how can anything flow out of the ice at all?
The answer came from under the glacier.
In 2017, a team used radar to look through Taylor Glacier. They found a hidden network of rivers and a lake underneath it, all filled with very salty, iron-rich water.
Salt keeps water from freezing. And when salty water does start to freeze, it releases heat, which melts more ice around it. So the water keeps moving.
When this iron-rich water reaches the air, the iron reacts with oxygen. It turns red, the same way metal turns red when it rusts.
That makes Taylor Glacier the coldest glacier known to have water constantly flowing through it.
Verdict: solved. It's salty, iron-rich water, rusting in the open air.
Number three: the hole in the ice.
Every winter, the sea around Antarctica freezes over. The sea ice covers an area about twice the size of the continental United States.
Near the coast, openings in the ice are normal. Strong winds push the ice away from land.
But in 2016 and 2017, a huge hole opened far out at sea, hundreds of kilometers from the coast, over water thousands of meters deep.
It grew nearly twice the size of Wales. And it stayed open for weeks.
This kind of opening is called a polynya. This one formed over an underwater mountain called Maud Rise.
It had been seen before, in the 1970s, when the first satellites that could see sea ice were launched. But after that, it showed up only rarely.
Scientists knew that warm, salty water from deep down was rising and melting the ice. But there was a problem. Melting ice makes the surface water fresher, and that should stop the mixing.
In their words: there must be an additional input of salt from somewhere.
In 2024, a team from the University of Southampton, the University of Gothenburg, and UC San Diego found it.
Swirling currents carried salt onto the top of Maud Rise. Then a process called Ekman transport, where water moves at an angle to the wind, pushed that salt to the spot where the hole first opened.
They called it the essential missing ingredient.
Verdict: solved. Wind, currents, and an underwater mountain.
Number four: the map that shows Antarctica without ice.
In 1513, the Ottoman admiral Piri Reis drew a world map. Only a part of it survives today.
In the 1960s, a writer named Charles Hapgood claimed the bottom of this map showed the coast of Antarctica, without its ice. That would mean someone mapped Antarctica long before anyone was known to have seen it.
The idea spread through books about lost civilizations and ancient astronauts.
But historians see it differently. Piri Reis wrote that he built his map from older maps, including one by Christopher Columbus.
And the coastline at the bottom can be explained as South America, bent to fit the edge of the page.
Historian Gregory McIntosh compared it with other maps from that century. His conclusion: it's not the most accurate map of the sixteenth century. Many maps from the same century were far better.
Verdict: solved. It's an early map of South America, not ice-free Antarctica.
Number five: the lake under the ice.
Deep under the ice sheet, there's a lake about the size of Lake Ontario. It's called Lake Vostok.
It sits under about four kilometers of ice, and it may have been sealed off for around fifteen million years.
Could anything live down there, in the dark, under all that pressure?
In February 2012, a Russian team finally drilled through to the lake.
Scientists had already found microbes in ice that froze onto the bottom of the ice sheet, straight from the lake water.
Then in 2013, a study claimed much more. It reported genetic traces of over 3,500 kinds of life, including signs of complex animals.
Other experts were skeptical. The old ice cores were drilled to study climate, not life, and the equipment wasn't sterile. The drilling fluid itself could have carried DNA from the surface.
The study's own leader said: time will tell if we're right or we're wrong.
Verdict: best guess. Probably some microbes. The bigger claims are not proven.
Number six: the signals from inside the Earth.
ANITA was a NASA experiment. Radio antennas hung from a balloon, flying about 37 kilometers above Antarctica.
It listened for radio flashes from tiny particles hitting the ice.
Most signals came down from the sky and bounced off the ice. But a few did something strange. They seemed to come up out of the ice, at steep angles, like 30 degrees below the surface.
To do that, the signal would have had to travel through thousands of kilometers of rock first. And the rock should have absorbed it.
Some wondered if this was a sign of new physics.
So in 2025, scientists checked fifteen years of data from the Pierre Auger Observatory in Argentina, one of the biggest particle detectors in the world. If these events were real particles coming up through the Earth, Auger should have seen many of them.
It didn't.
Physicist Stephanie Wissel said: we still don't actually have an explanation for what those anomalies are.
A new balloon experiment, called PUEO, is being built to look again.
Verdict: still unknown.
So, let's check the scoreboard.
The pyramid: solved. A mountain.
Blood Falls: solved. Iron-rich water, rusting.
The hole in the ice: solved. Wind, currents, and an underwater mountain.
The ice-free map: solved. South America.
Life in Lake Vostok: best guess. Probably microbes.
The signals from inside the Earth: still unknown.
Four solved, one best guess, and one we still can't explain. Antarctica gets a lot of wild theories. The real answers are often stranger.
