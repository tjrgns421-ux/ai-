You'd never guess what made this sound.
In 1997, it was heard across the Pacific, over five thousand kilometers away. What was it, actually?
That's the first of six strange sounds recorded in the ocean.
For each one, we'll hear the real recording, look at what scientists found, and give an honest verdict: solved, best guess, or still unknown.
One quick note. Most of these sounds are too low for human ears. So the recordings you'll hear have been sped up, exactly as the scientists published them.
First, how do you even hear the ocean?
During the Cold War, the U.S. Navy built a network of underwater microphones, called hydrophones, to listen for Soviet submarines. It was called SOSUS.
After the Cold War ended, the Navy partly opened that system to scientists. And NOAA, the U.S. ocean agency, added hydrophones of its own, to listen for undersea earthquakes, volcanoes, ice and whales.
Sound travels incredibly well in water. So these microphones started picking up things nobody expected.
Number one: the Bloop.
In the summer of 1997, NOAA's hydrophones in the Pacific caught an extremely loud, very low sound. It rose in pitch over about a minute.
It was loud enough to be picked up by sensors more than five thousand kilometers apart.
The source was traced to a remote corner of the South Pacific, far from any coast.
Here's what it sounds like, sped up sixteen times.
Because it was so loud, people wondered if it came from an animal. Maybe a giant one that nobody had ever seen.
For years, the Bloop was one of the ocean's most famous mysteries.
Then the answer came from the ice.
In 2008, NOAA scientists were tracking a huge iceberg, called A53a, as it broke apart near South Georgia Island.
As the iceberg cracked and fractured, their hydrophones recorded icequakes. And the icequakes looked very similar to the Bloop.
NOAA's conclusion: the Bloop was consistent with icequakes from large icebergs, most likely somewhere between the Bransfield Strait and the Ross Sea, off Antarctica.
Not a monster. Just ice, cracking on a scale that's hard to imagine.
Verdict: solved. It was breaking ice.
Number two: Julia and the Slow Down.
On the first of March, 1999, NOAA's hydrophones in the eastern Pacific recorded a long, moaning sound. Scientists named it Julia.
Here it is, sped up sixteen times.
Two years earlier, in May 1997, they had recorded another strange one. It slowly dropped in pitch over about seven minutes. They called it the Slow Down.
Both sounds turned out to have the same kind of source.
A huge drifting iceberg runs into the seafloor. As it scrapes along the bottom and slowly grinds to a stop, it groans.
For Julia, NOAA says the most likely source is a large iceberg that ran aground off Antarctica.
And for the Slow Down, the falling pitch tells the story: the iceberg is slowing down as it drags across the seafloor.
Verdict: solved. Icebergs running aground.
Number three: the Bio-Duck.
In the 1960s, submarine crews in the Southern Ocean started hearing a strange, repeating sound. It reminded them of a duck. So they called it the Bio-Duck.
Here's a NOAA recording of it. This one is not sped up.
For about fifty years, nobody could say what made it. It was so regular that it almost sounded mechanical. Some even suspected secret military devices.
But there were clues. The sound came back every year, around the same months, near Antarctica. That looked a lot like a migrating animal.
In February 2013, a research team took a small boat into Wilhelmina Bay, off the Antarctic Peninsula. Using a long pole, they stuck suction-cup recorders onto two Antarctic minke whales.
They were the first acoustic tags ever attached to this species.
And on one of the tags, just before the whale dived to feed, the Bio-Duck sound appeared.
The study came out in 2014. The lead scientist, Denise Risch of NOAA, said her heart jumped a little when she found it.
We still don't know exactly why the whales make it. But we know who does.
Verdict: solved. It's Antarctic minke whales.
Number four: the Whistle.
On the seventh of July, 1997, a NOAA hydrophone in the eastern Pacific recorded a single, wavering tone. They named it the Whistle.
Here it is, sped up sixteen times.
NOAA says it's similar to sounds recorded from undersea volcanoes while they were erupting.
The same array had picked up volcano sounds before, from the Mariana Islands, about fourteen thousand kilometers away.
But there's a problem. To find where a sound came from, you need at least three hydrophones. The Whistle was heard by only one.
So we know what it sounds like. We just can't point to the volcano.
Verdict: best guess. Probably an undersea eruption, but the source was never located.
Number five: the Upsweep.
When NOAA started listening to the Navy's hydrophones in August 1991, this sound was already there.
It's a long series of rising sweeps, each lasting several seconds. And it's loud enough to be heard across the Pacific.
Here it is, sped up twenty times.
At first, some thought it came from whales. But the sound was too regular for an animal.
It also changes with the seasons. It usually peaks in spring and fall. But no one knows whether the source itself changes, or just the way sound travels through the ocean.
Scientists traced it roughly to a spot in the far South Pacific, near an area of suspected volcanic activity.
And here's the strange part. Since 1991, it has been getting quieter. But it hasn't stopped.
NOAA's own description ends with this: the origin of the sound is unresolved.
Verdict: still unknown. Volcanic activity is the leading idea, but nobody has found the source.
Number six: the loneliest whale.
In 1989, a team from the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution heard a whale call in the North Pacific. It had all the patterns of a whale.
But it was at fifty-two hertz. Blue and fin whales call much lower, at around fifteen to twenty-five hertz.
Here's the call, sped up ten times.
After the Navy opened its hydrophones, the team tracked this one voice every year.
It showed up between August and December, roamed from California to the Aleutian Islands of Alaska, and traveled up to eleven thousand kilometers in a single season.
And in twelve years of careful listening, they found only one source with that call each season.
In their words: it is perhaps difficult to accept that if this was a whale, there could have been only one of this kind in this large oceanic expanse.
The press called it the loneliest whale in the world. It even inspired songs, including one by BTS, called Whalien 52.
But is it really alone? In 2010, sensors off California picked up calls suggesting there might be more than one whale calling at fifty-two hertz.
So what is it? The scientists suggested it might have a malformation, or be a hybrid of two species, like a blue whale and a fin whale. A 2021 documentary filmed a possible hybrid. But no one has ever confirmed which animal is making the call.
Verdict: still unknown. We know it's a whale. We just don't know what kind.
So, let's check the scoreboard.
The Bloop: solved. Breaking ice.
Julia and the Slow Down: solved. Icebergs running aground.
The Bio-Duck: solved. Antarctic minke whales.
The Whistle: best guess. An undersea volcano.
The Upsweep: still unknown.
The 52-hertz whale: still unknown.
Three solved, one best guess, and two we still can't explain. The ocean covers most of our planet, and we're still learning to hear it.
