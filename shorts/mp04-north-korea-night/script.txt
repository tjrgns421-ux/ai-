You've seen North Korea at night: a black hole next to South Korea. But NASA's 2026 data shows lights it never had in 2012. What changed?
NASA calls it almost completely dark. So is North Korea really dark at night? And why is it changing now?
Three questions. How dark is it, really? What are the few lights? And where is the new light coming from?
First, the famous picture.
In 2002, U.S. Defense Secretary Donald Rumsfeld showed a satellite image of the Korean Peninsula at night.
According to NPR, he said: "South Korea is filled with lights and energy and vitality and a booming economy. North Korea is dark."
Twelve years later, astronauts on the International Space Station took this photo.
NASA's description: North Korea is almost completely dark compared to neighboring South Korea and China.
The darkened land, NASA says, looks like a patch of water joining the Yellow Sea to the Sea of Japan.
Even the coastline is hard to find.
And Pyongyang, a city of more than three million people, looks like a small island.
NASA says its light is about the same as the smaller towns in South Korea.
So why so dark? Start with electricity.
NASA's caption gives per person power use: about 10,000 kilowatt hours a year in South Korea. In North Korea, 739.
About one-fourteenth.
And the gap hasn't closed. According to South Korea's statistics agency, North Korea generated 25.3 billion kilowatt hours in 2024. That's just 4.2 percent of what the South generated.
National Geographic traces the darkness back to the mid-1990s, when fuel stopped flowing from the collapsed Soviet Union.
Even Kim Jong Un admitted the problem. In his 2014 New Year's address, he told the country to save even a single watt of electricity.
That winter, according to the Korea JoongAng Daily, Pyongyang went through one of its worst blackout crises in years.
So the famous photo checks out. North Korea is dark. But not all of it.
Part two. The few lights.
In January 2024, another astronaut photographed the peninsula from the space station.
NASA's caption says only two small clusters of light are easily visible in North Korea.
One is Pyongyang, the capital.
The other is a place called Yangdok, in the middle of the country.
So what's in Yangdok? A factory? A power plant?
NASA doesn't say what the light is. But here's what North Korea built there.
According to the Korea Times, the Yangdok Hot Spring resort began operating in January 2020. Kim Jong Un visited it several times while it was being built.
We can't confirm the light is the resort. But in a country short of electricity, one of only two bright spots is home to a brand new spa.
And Pyongyang isn't lit evenly either.
National Geographic noted that some buildings, including the Juche Tower in the center of the city, stay lit around the clock.
So North Korea isn't uniformly dark. The lights are where the government puts them.
But here's what most people don't know. The picture is changing.
Part three. Is it getting brighter?
We wanted to check this ourselves.
NASA publishes nightly light data from the Suomi NPP satellite. We pulled it for the Korean Peninsula, for three October nights in every year from 2012 to 2026.
Then we drew the border, and counted how much of each country's land was lit.
Here's 2012. South Korea: about 14 percent of its land lit. North Korea: less than a tenth of one percent.
For years, North Korea barely moved.
Then, after 2022, it started climbing.
By 2025, North Korea's lit area was four to five times bigger than in 2012.
South Korea grew too, by about half. So part of this is the satellite data itself, not just the ground.
But North Korea grew much faster.
To be clear, this is our measurement, with a simple method. So we looked for other people who measured it.
In June 2026, the Korea JoongAng Daily reported on a study by researchers at KAIST, working with Germany's Max Planck Institute.
They tracked night lights in 178 North Korean cities and counties, from January 2022 to October 2025.
On average, North Korea got 25.9 percent brighter.
Pyongyang got 116.5 percent brighter. More than double.
And in 2025, Pyongyang was nearly nine times brighter than the average of the rest of the country.
A second check. In August 2026, Daily NK compared monthly satellite averages for Pyongyang from 2020 to 2026.
It found the city's lit area grew about 2.4 times.
That's three measurements, three different methods. All going the same direction.
So where is the new light coming from?
The study's answer points north. To Russia.
According to the JoongAng Daily, the brightening began around the time South Korea's military says North Korea started supplying weapons to Russia, in mid-2022.
Intelligence authorities estimate Russia supplied 1.5 million barrels of refined petroleum products in 2024 alone. The UN cap is 500,000.
The researchers are careful. They say they can't prove Russian support caused the brighter nights. But they call the timing hard to see as a coincidence.
And look at which places brightened.
After Pyongyang, the biggest gains were in Wonsan, Kim Jong Un's hometown and a new beach resort, and in Sinuiju, near the Chinese border.
Arms factory towns brightened too. Kusong, home to an ammunition plant, was eighth.
The researchers describe it as an electricity dictatorship pipeline: power flows to Pyongyang and the arms industry first.
And the rest of the country? Still dark.
As of 2024, Seoul was 14.3 times brighter than Pyongyang.
And Pyongyang was the only place in North Korea brighter than one of South Korea's darkest counties, Yeongyang, home to about 15,000 people.
So, the check.
Claim: North Korea is dark at night.
Mostly true. By our count, more than 99 percent of its land is still unlit on a typical October night.
Claim: North Korea is completely dark, except for Pyongyang.
Not quite. A spa resort showed up from space. A hometown beach resort is shining. And arms factory towns are getting brighter.
Claim: nothing has changed since that famous photo.
False. Between 2022 and 2025, Pyongyang more than doubled in brightness.
Verdict: dark, but not frozen. The lights are coming back. Just not for everyone.
