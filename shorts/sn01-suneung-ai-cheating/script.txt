On exam day, South Korea stops planes from taking off and landing. Not for a storm. For a test.
More than five hundred fifty thousand people sit the Suneung, Korea's college entrance exam. And this year, officials are on high alert for one thing: AI.
It started with a different exam.
The top scorer on Korea's national tax accountant exam admitted, in a video on his lawyer's YouTube channel, that he cheated using AI.
He says a hidden camera captured the questions, an AI tool produced the answers, and he received them during the exam.
According to Korean media, he got one hundred fifty-nine of one hundred sixty questions right in the first round.
He later gave police a written confession, and police have searched his home. The investigation is ongoing.
And it may not stop there. Korean media report the investigation could expand to other national exams.
Days later, all eyes turned to the Suneung.
To see why, you need to know how big this day is.
The exam runs from eight forty in the morning to five forty-five in the evening.
Offices are asked to start work later, and the stock market opens late, so students can get to their exam sites.
In the Seoul area, subways run more often in the morning.
Police often escort students who are running late.
And during the English listening test, planes are not allowed to take off or land. Military drills with artillery and tanks are put on hold too.
Blip has never seen a country this quiet.
The Associated Press reports that in Korea, jobs, social standing, and even marriage prospects can depend on which university a person attends.
So when AI cheating makes the news, the whole country pays attention.
Some lawmakers even called for postponing the exam.
The education minister said no. He told a hearing that a delay would cause an even larger disruption for students.
Instead, this year, the new rules target AI.
Proctors will inspect every test-taker's glasses, and shirt buttons will be covered with stickers, to block hidden cameras.
Proctors will also use metal detectors on test-takers who act suspiciously.
Phones, smart glasses, smart watches, anything with a camera, must be handed in. Not handing them in counts as cheating, even if they are never used.
About eight thousand four hundred extra supervisors are being deployed.
On the morning of the exam, an emergency alert will make nearly every phone in the country buzz for about ten seconds, to expose hidden devices.
The president also told officials to consider sending more alerts at unpredictable times during the exam.
The prime minister said the exam ban for cheaters will be extended from one year to ten.
College students retaking the exam who are caught cheating will be expelled from their current schools.
She also said companies that run AI chatbots have agreed to help with investigations after the exam.
Is it too much? The vice education minister said: we believe we are in a situation where excessive measures are necessary.
And this isn't the first warning. In June, Korea reported its first cheating cases using AI smart glasses, on the TOEIC English test.
In October, smart glasses were also caught on some university essay exams.
Checks at the door might work for one day.
But every school now faces a bigger question. If AI can answer the test, what should the test be testing?
To everyone sitting the Suneung: good luck. You've got this. No AI needed.
