If you talk like a South Korean in North Korea, a 2023 law could reportedly cost you six years. But why?
It's the same language. Same alphabet, same grammar. Until 1945, it was one country.
So, three questions. What does this law actually say? How different are the two Koreans, really? And why would a government go after the way people talk?
First, the law.
In January 2023, North Korea adopted the Pyongyang Cultural Language Protection Act.
Daily NK, a Seoul-based outlet that covers North Korea, says it obtained the full text. Five chapters. Sixty-five articles.
Sixty-five articles. About words.
What does it target? Asia Economy reports South Korean-style speech and loanwords, the kind people pick up from South Korean dramas and movies.
Citing South Korea's spy agency, it lists the words. Calling your husband "oppa". Calling your boyfriend "jagiya".
And according to a later Asia Economy report, even names like Sora and Gahee were treated as South Korean, and people were made to change them.
And the penalties? According to the text Daily NK published, Article 58 says speaking or writing that way means six years or more of reform through labor.
Article 59 says teaching it means ten years or more. In serious cases, life, or death.
That's the text as reported. How it's enforced, nobody outside North Korea can check. So every penalty on screen says who reported it.
But here's the strange part. If both sides speak Korean, what is there to ban?
To answer that, go back to the split.
After 1945, the peninsula was divided. The South's standard language was based on the speech of Seoul.
Then, in 1966, a talk by Kim Il Sung set up the North's own standard. Centered on Pyongyang speech, and independent from the South's.
It got a name: munhwaeo. The cultured language.
Which, if you're from Seoul, is a little rude.
And here's the twist. Linguist S. Robert Ramsey points out that the North's standard is itself based on the old speech of Seoul.
So the North declared independence from Seoul, in a language built on Seoul.
Then it started cleaning house. A campaign to replace foreign words and Chinese-based words with native Korean ones.
In 1988, the journal of South Korea's national language institute published a list of what the North came up with.
Ice cream? "Eoreum-bosungi". Roughly, ice fluff.
Caramel? "Gireum-satang". Oil candy.
A knock on the door? "Son-gicheok". Literally, your hand saying "I'm here".
Honestly? Some of these are better.
Meanwhile, the South went the other way. According to Daily NK, words like computer, cafe and internet are everywhere in the South, but virtually unknown in the North.
You can even see the split in print. The North's main newspaper is the Rodong Sinmun. Rodong, with an R, means labor.
In South Korean spelling, the same word starts with an N. Nodong.
Same word. Same meaning. Different first letter. Like British and American spelling, but with a border in the middle.
So, how different is it, really? This is where it gets complicated.
North Koreans who resettle in the South start at Hanawon, a government center. A 2005 Korea JoongAng Daily report says it spends three months on adjustment, including lessons on South Korean pronunciation and foreign words.
But a study released in 2007 by South Korea's National Institute of Korean Language asked a hundred defectors how long it took before they stopped feeling the language difference.
The biggest group, thirty-two percent, said three years or more.
In the vocabulary test, among the words defectors got wrong most often, about eighty percent were loanwords.
But the same study has a twist.
Asked if the language gap made daily life hard, forty-three percent said no. Only twenty-one percent said yes.
And asked if they would try to talk like South Koreans, sixty-nine percent said no.
So the gap is real. But it's not a wall. It's more like a very annoying fence. And plenty of people are fine standing on their side of it.
Which brings us back to the big question. If people can mostly understand each other, why ban the South Korean way of talking?
Because the words give away where you got them.
Asia Economy, citing South Korea's spy agency, says young North Koreans were picking up Seoul speech by secretly watching South Korean dramas.
And watching those dramas was already dangerous. According to the democracy group International IDEA, a 2020 law in the North authorized the death penalty for distributing K-dramas.
So the language law closes a gap. A drama can be hidden. A habit in your voice is harder to hide.
Not every link is broken, though. The two Koreas have a joint project to compile one dictionary of their shared language.
According to Seoul's 2026 unification white paper, the South put two point four eight six billion won into it last year.
One dictionary. Two standards. Still being written.
So, the check.
Claim: North and South Koreans now speak two different languages.
Vocabulary? Clearly drifting. Loanwords are the biggest gap, and the North widened it on purpose.
But the alphabet, the grammar, and the base of both standards are shared. And most defectors in that study said the gap did not make daily life hard.
And the penalties? They come from a law text published by Daily NK. How they're enforced, we can't verify.
Verdict: half true. One language, two dictionaries, and one side that is trying very hard to keep them apart.
