If you talk like a South Korean in North Korea, a 2023 law could reportedly cost you six years. But why?
It's the same language. Same alphabet, same grammar. Until 1945, it was one country.
So, three questions. What does this law actually say? How different are the two Koreans, really? And why would a government go after the way people talk?
First, the law.
In January 2023, North Korea adopted the Pyongyang Cultured Language Protection Act.
According to Daily NK, which says it obtained the full text, it has five chapters and sixty-five articles.
Sixty-five articles. About words.
What does it ban? Asia Economy reports that it targets South Korean-style speech, the kind people pick up from dramas and movies.
Calling your boyfriend "oppa". Calling your partner "jagiya". Even names that sound too South Korean.
And the penalties? This is where the reports split.
Asia Economy says at least six years in prison for using that speech, and death for teaching it.
Daily NK says the maximum penalty is execution.
We could not check the law itself. So on screen, every penalty you see says who reported it.
But here's the strange part. If both sides speak Korean, what is there to ban?
To answer that, go back to the split.
After 1945, the peninsula was divided. And each side picked its own standard.
The South built its standard on the speech of Seoul.
The North went a different way. In 1966, it made its standard official, based on Pyongyang speech.
It gave it a name: munhwaeo. The cultured language.
Which, if you're from Seoul, is a little rude.
And the North started cleaning house. Foreign loanwords, out. Native Korean words, in.
Shampoo became "meori mul binu". Hair water soap.
Juice became "danmul". Sweet water.
Honestly? Sweet water is not wrong.
Meanwhile, the South went the other way. English words poured in. Computer, coffee, shopping, meeting. All in daily Korean.
So after seventy years, the gap is not really grammar. It's vocabulary.
Here's one rule you can actually see. In the South, some words drop the R sound at the start. In the North, they keep it.
That's why the North's main newspaper is the Rodong Sinmun. In the South, the same word is "nodong".
And it's why a family name can be Ri in the North but Lee in the South.
Same letters. Different rule. Like British and American spelling, but with a border in the middle.
So, how different is it, really? This is where it gets complicated.
North Koreans who resettle in the South get language classes at Hanawon, the government resettlement center, according to Daily NK. The classes cover pronunciation, loanwords and basic English.
Linguists quoted by PRI's The World said it takes about two years before many defectors feel comfortable in conversation.
And an older study commissioned by South Korea's National Institute of Korean Language found the hardest words were mostly loanwords. Among the words defectors struggled with most, about eighty percent were loanwords.
But the same study has a twist.
When asked if the language gap made daily life hard, about forty-three percent said no. Only about twenty-one percent said yes.
So it's real. But it's not a wall. It's more like a very annoying fence.
Some linguists even argue that North Korea's standard is still firmly rooted in Seoul speech. Scholars disagree on how far the two have drifted.
Which brings us back to the big question. If people can mostly understand each other, why ban the South Korean way of talking?
Because the words give away where you got them.
You don't learn "oppa" the South Korean way from the state newspaper. You learn it from a drama.
And watching those dramas was already a crime. A 2020 law in the North authorized the death penalty for distributing South Korean video, according to the democracy group International IDEA.
So the language law closes a gap. A drama can be hidden. A habit in your voice is harder to hide.
Not every link is broken, though. The two Koreas started a joint dictionary of their shared language.
In its 2026 unification white paper, Seoul's unification ministry lists two point four eight six billion won spent on that project.
One dictionary. Two standards. Still being written.
So, the check.
Claim: North and South Koreans now speak two different languages.
Vocabulary? Clearly drifting. Loanwords are the biggest gap, and the North made that gap on purpose.
But grammar, the alphabet, and most daily words are still shared. And most defectors in that study said the gap did not make life hard.
And the penalties? Reported, but not something we could verify.
Verdict: half true. One language, two dictionaries, and one side that is trying very hard to keep them apart.
