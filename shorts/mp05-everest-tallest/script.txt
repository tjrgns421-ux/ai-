You learned Everest is the tallest at 8,849 meters. NOAA says it's not. And it's never been the closest to the stars. So which mountain wins?
It depends on the ruler. So we checked three of them.
So, three questions. How tall is Everest, really? Which mountain beats it from the bottom? And which one beats it from the center of the Earth?
First, the number itself.
In 2020, China and Nepal announced Everest's new official height together: 8,848.86 meters.
According to Al Jazeera, that was a little higher than Nepal's old figure, and about four meters higher than China's.
Wait. Four meters? For the most famous mountain on Earth?
Measuring it has never been simple. Let's go back to the start.
In 1849 and 1850, British surveyors in India measured a peak they called Peak XV.
They weren't anywhere near it. According to the Himalayan Journal, they observed it from stations on the plains, about 110 miles away.
From there, Peak XV didn't even look special. It was hidden behind lower peaks. At the time, people believed Kanchenjunga was the highest mountain in the world.
Six stations measured it. And they got six different answers.
28,991 feet. 29,005. 29,001. 28,998. 29,026. And 28,990.
The surveyors took the average: 29,002 feet.
And here's the hard part. The air bends light. For Everest, the journal says, the correction for that bending was 1,375 feet.
It took until 1852 to see that Peak XV was higher than anything else measured. Only in 1865 was the figure accepted as reliable. And the peak was named after Sir George Everest, who had retired from the survey years earlier.
Then, in the early 1950s, the Survey of India came up with 8,848 meters. That number stuck for decades.
But not everywhere. In 2007, NPR said Everest was 29,035 feet. That's about 8,850 meters.
And after a big earthquake in 2015, Al Jazeera reports, people wondered if the mountain had changed height.
Nepal sent its own team to the summit in 2019, the first time it measured the mountain on its own. China sent one in 2020.
Result: 8,848.86 meters. Settled, at least for now.
So Everest is the highest point above sea level. NOAA agrees.
But "highest" and "tallest" aren't the same question.
Part two. From the bottom.
What if you measure a mountain from its base, not from the sea?
NOAA's answer: the tallest mountain on Earth is Mauna Kea, a volcano in Hawaii.
Above the water, it's only about 4,200 meters.
But it starts deep under the Pacific. NOAA says it rises more than 10,210 meters from base to peak.
That's more than a kilometer taller than Everest's 8,849.
So Everest loses? Not so fast.
Here's the catch. Where exactly is the base of a mountain?
According to Wikipedia's summary of the sources, the answers range from 9,966 meters, measured to the deepest point of the trench around Hawaii, to 17,205 meters, measured to the root of the mountain deep underground.
And Mauna Kea is so heavy that, together with its neighbor Mauna Loa, it pushes the ocean floor down by about six kilometers.
So "tallest" depends on where you start counting. Using NOAA's figure, Mauna Kea wins.
And there's a third ruler.
Part three. From the center of the Earth.
The Earth isn't a perfect ball. It spins, and it bulges at the equator.
Sea level at the equator is about 21 kilometers farther from Earth's center than sea level at the poles. That's by the standard model of the Earth's shape.
So a mountain near the equator gets a head start.
Meet Chimborazo, a volcano in Ecuador, just one degree south of the equator.
It's 6,263 meters above sea level. More than two and a half kilometers lower than Everest.
But measured from the center of the Earth, NOAA says Chimborazo's summit is more than 2,072 meters farther out than Everest's.
NOAA calls it the closest point on Earth to the stars.
And again, the sources disagree on how much farther.
NOAA: over 2,072 meters. The Library of Congress: 2,163 meters. Wikipedia: 2,168 meters. And NPR, in 2007, quoted a figure of 1.5 miles. That's about 2,400 meters.
So we did the math ourselves.
We used the standard shape of the Earth that GPS uses, and the official heights of both summits.
Everest's summit: about 6,382.3 kilometers from the center.
Chimborazo's summit: about 6,384.4 kilometers.
The difference: about 2,080 meters.
That's close to NOAA's number. It's a simple calculation, so a few tens of meters either way. But the answer doesn't change.
Chimborazo is farther out. By about two kilometers.
Here's the fun part. People used to think Chimborazo was the highest mountain in the world.
According to the Library of Congress, Alexander von Humboldt tried to climb it in 1802. He reached 19,286 feet, higher than any European had gone before, and still fell about a thousand feet short.
The first recorded ascent came in 1880, by Edward Whymper.
It lost the title of highest. But it kept a stranger one.
So, the check.
Claim one: Everest is the highest mountain above sea level.
True. 8,848.86 meters, by the 2020 joint measurement.
Claim two: Everest is the tallest mountain on Earth.
False, by NOAA's definition. Mauna Kea is taller from base to peak.
Claim three: the top of Everest is the closest you can get to space.
False. Chimborazo's summit is about two kilometers farther from Earth's center.
Verdict: Everest is the highest. It is not the tallest. And it is not the farthest out.
Three rulers. Three winners.
