Three questions. What actually changed? Does it really beat Claude? And which one should you use, starting today?
On November seventeenth, OpenAI released GPT-6.2 Astra and GPT-6.2 Sol.
Astra is the big one, built for complex professional work.
Sol is what OpenAI calls its go-to model for everyday work.
If you're keeping track, OpenAI now has Astra, Sol, Terra and Luna. That's not a model lineup. That's a solar system.
Both are live in ChatGPT Work and Codex, for Plus, Pro, Enterprise and Edu.
On Enterprise and Edu, your workspace admin has to switch them on first. So if you don't see them, go bother your IT person. Nicely.
Astra also arrives less than two months after OpenAI called off GPT-6.1 Astra, because testing found it didn't always stay within scope and authorization.
In other words, it didn't stay in its lane. Blip would never. Blip asks first.
Astra's pitch is office work. OpenAI says it was trained to make slides, spreadsheets and documents that follow professional conventions.
It can even match a slide template from a file or a screenshot, down to the exact font size.
Finally, an AI that cares about fonts as much as your manager does.
One early tester, an accounting startup called Basis, says its average time on long accounting work fell by half.
Now the price. On OpenAI's pricing page, Astra costs eight dollars per million input tokens, and forty per million output.
Sol costs two and ten.
So per token, Astra is four times the price.
To be fair, that's twenty percent cheaper than the Astra before it. Expensive, but on sale.
And here's the part that got less spotlight.
On a coding benchmark called FrontierCode 2.0, OpenAI says Sol came within half a point of Astra, at about one fifth of the cost.
You will not feel half a point. Your wallet will feel five times.
OpenAI also says Sol lets you finish more tasks on the same paid plan than the last Sol did.
So, does Astra really beat Claude? Quick detour. Thirty seconds on benchmarks.
A benchmark is just a test. Same questions for every model, then you compare scores.
The catch: there are hundreds of tests, and every company picks which ones go in the launch post.
It's like bringing home only the report card with the A on it. Not lying. Just curating.
OpenAI's Claude claim comes from one test, Agents' Last Exam, which measures multistep professional work.
On that test, OpenAI says Astra finished five and a half points ahead of Claude Fable 5.2, at five percent of the cost.
To check it, you want an independent scorekeeper. Artificial Analysis runs a whole bundle of tests and publishes the results.
On its Intelligence Index, at max settings, Claude Fable 5.2 scores sixty-five.
GPT-6.2 Astra scores sixty-two. Sol scores fifty-seven.
So overall, Claude is still on top. Astra wins the test OpenAI picked, not the whole report card.
Now look at cost per task on the same index.
Sol: about a dollar. Astra: four dollars. Fable 5.2: twenty-six fifty.
So here, Astra costs about fifteen percent of Claude. Not five. But still a lot cheaper. That part is real.
Artificial Analysis also flags Astra as a bit verbose. It likes to talk. Blip respects that.
So which one should you use?
Start with Sol. Chat, quick answers, everyday coding, drafts and emails. It's cheaper, and on coding it's basically tied.
Switch to Astra when the output is the job: a slide deck, a spreadsheet, a legal or finance document that has to look like a pro made it.
And if Claude already does that work for you, this launch alone isn't a reason to switch. It's a reason to test both on your own tasks.
